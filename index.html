St. Charles County, Missouri

For decades, the word was secret.
It's time for it to be understood.

Weldon Spring made explosives for World War II and processed uranium for the Cold War. Its waste is now sealed in a 41-acre engineered disposal cell you can climb. Most people don't know it's there, or that the main cleanup was finished back in 2001.

03 · Before the fences

Howell, Toonerville and Hamburg

Before World War II, this was farmland with three small rural towns.

In 1940, President Franklin D. Roosevelt ordered the government to acquire 17,232 acres for an ordnance works. The 576 residents got 45 days to leave their homes, churches and land. They also lost their cemeteries, and with them much of the physical record of the towns.

Today a plaque on top of the disposal cell recognizes the displaced communities.

“45 days to leave their homes, churches, and lives.”

04 · Timeline

From explosives to uranium to prairie

  1. 1941
    Weldon Spring Ordnance Works opens. From 1941 to 1945 the Army made TNT and DNT here. The work left nitroaromatic contamination in soil, sediment and some offsite springs.
  2. 1941–45
    The quarry. Limestone was quarried for construction, and the quarry was then used to burn explosive waste and dump TNT rubble. Nitroaromatics reached the soil and the groundwater.
  3. 1956
    The Atomic Energy Commission (AEC) moves in. After what the Army called “considerable” decontamination, 205 acres went to the AEC for the Weldon Spring Uranium Feed Materials Plant. Another 14.88 acres followed in 1964.
  4. 1957–66
    Uranium processing. The plant turned uranium ore into pure uranium trioxide, intermediate compounds and uranium metal. It also processed thorium. Waste went into four raffinate pits.
  5. 1960–69
    Quarry becomes a dump. The Army transferred the quarry to the AEC, which disposed of uranium and thorium residues there from 1963 to 1969, some contained and some not.
  6. 1966–67
    Defoliant plan, then cancelled. The plant went back to the Army, which planned to make defoliant there. Equipment was dumped into Raffinate Pit 4. When the project was cancelled, 50.65 acres containing the pits returned to the AEC.
  7. 1985
    DOE takes over. The Army did some decontamination in 1984 and transferred the site to the Department of Energy in 1985. By then it had sat for about 20 years with broken walls, shattered windows and a broken water main that let more contamination leak out of the buildings.
  8. 1986–2001
    The cleanup. The Weldon Spring Site Remedial Action Project demolished the plant, drained the pits and built the disposal cell (1997–2001).
  9. 2002
    Interpretive Center opens. The site begins long-term monitoring under the DOE Office of Legacy Management.
  10. 2025
    Recognition. Congress expands the Radiation Exposure Compensation Act (RECA) to cover people exposed to Manhattan Project waste in the St. Louis region, including the Weldon Spring area.

06 · The part most people never hear

There is already a solution in place

The waste from the plant, the pits and the quarry was dug up and sealed in one engineered, multi-layered disposal cell.

Construction ran from 1997 to 2001. Since then the Department of Energy has inspected and monitored the site every year, and EPA and the Missouri Department of Natural Resources review it every five years. Groundwater beneath the site is handled through monitored natural attenuation: contaminants are tracked as they break down and dilute, and groundwater use there is restricted.

08 · The bigger picture

Three area hotspots, one atomic legacy

All three trace back to the same source: Mallinckrodt Chemical Works, which refined uranium for the Manhattan Project and later the AEC. Its waste was moved, stored and dumped around the region for decades.

Remediated · monitoring

Weldon Spring

St. Charles County · DOE Office of Legacy Management

The former ordnance works and uranium plant. The waste is sealed in the disposal cell, and the site is now prairie, trails and an education center. The remaining issue is groundwater under the site.

Cleanup begins 2027

West Lake Landfill

Bridgeton · EPA Superfund

Radioactive waste from the St. Louis Airport Site was brought here in the 1970s. The site was added to the Superfund list in 1990. A nearby underground landfill fire raised fears that it could reach the waste.

Active cleanup

Coldwater Creek

North St. Louis County · Army Corps of Engineers (FUSRAP)

Drums of waste stored at the airport leaked into this 19-mile creek. Jana Elementary in Florissant closed in 2022. In 2026 the Army Corps demolished six homes in the Cades Cove neighborhood to reach contaminated soil underneath them.

09 · Next up

The EPA's West Lake cleanup

In the documentary Atomic Homefront, some Pattonville parents first heard about the problem when a letter came home saying their kids might have to shelter in place at school in an emergency. That is what happens when information arrives decades late.

The plan now is partial excavation of the radioactive material, offsite disposal and an engineered cover. In 2025 the EPA moved the start of excavation up from May 2029 to late 2027. Pre-excavation sampling was finished in March 2026. The EPA expects the cleanup to be complete by about 2038.

Follow progress on the EPA West Lake dashboard, the first Superfund site with a public tracking dashboard.

10 · In memory

They worked in secret, without knowing the danger

Beginning in 1942, workers at Mallinckrodt Chemical Works in downtown St. Louis refined the uranium used in the world's first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction and in the Manhattan Project. Many later processed uranium at Weldon Spring. They were sworn to secrecy and given little information about what they were handling. They became some of the most contaminated nuclear workers in history.

Many of them, and many of their neighbors, later developed cancer and other illnesses. Their families spent decades fighting for recognition. That fight helped expand federal compensation for nuclear workers through EEOICPA and, in 2025, for residents through RECA.

To the workers, the families of Howell, Toonerville and Hamburg, and the neighbors who paid for a secret they were never told.

Secrecy created the fear. Education can bring closure.

The Weldon Spring story isn't a cover-up still happening. It's a legacy that was kept quiet for too long. The danger was real, part of the solution is already in place, and some of the work is still ahead. People who know which is which can stop panicking and start asking better questions.
